Mountain GorillaGorilla beringei beringei

Field notes on one of the world's largest primates, and the closing window to keep it in the wild.

Critically endangered
I.

Where it lives

Mountain gorillas keep to the volcanic highlands of the Virunga Massif, where Rwanda, Uganda, and the Democratic Republic of Congo meet, and to the separate stronghold of Bwindi Impenetrable Forest. They range between 2,400 and 4,000 metres, moving through bamboo stands and mist-thick montane jungle that turns cold enough at night to see their breath.

Each troop is led by a dominant silverback and rarely strays far from a home range it knows in detail — the same fig trees, the same wallows, the same sheltered slopes, season after season.

II.

Why it's endangered

Two pressures sit above the rest: hunting and the steady loss of forest. Both trace back to the same root — a shrinking amount of undisturbed land for a species that needs a great deal of it.

Hunting

Gorillas are rarely the intended target, but snares set for antelope and other bushmeat close on them just the same. A snared hand or foot can be fatal without intervention.

Habitat loss

Farmland and settlement edge steadily into the forest margins, cutting territory into smaller, disconnected pieces and pushing troops into closer contact with people.

Disease

A 98% genetic overlap with humans works against them here — the common cold can become a serious respiratory illness in a gorilla, spread through routine contact with visitors and researchers.

Conflict

Decades of instability in parts of their range have made patrolling and monitoring dangerous, sometimes fatal work for the rangers who protect them.

III.

Population, in view

Roughly 1,100 mountain gorillas remain — up from a low near 250 in the 1980s. It is one of the few great ape populations currently trending upward, though the total is still small enough that a single bad season could reverse it.

IV.

How to protect it

The recovery so far didn't happen by accident. It came from specific, sustained work — the same work that still needs funding and attention today.

Fund ranger patrols

Anti-poaching teams check for snares daily. Every trap found before a troop passes through is a gorilla that doesn't need rescuing.

Hold the forest line

Enforced park boundaries and community land-use agreements keep farmland from creeping further into gorilla range.

Keep tourism strict

Trekking fees fund conservation directly, but only if visitor numbers, distances, and health checks stay tightly controlled.

Guard against disease

Masking near gorilla groups and turning away sick visitors and staff remains the simplest way to prevent an outbreak.

Support the organisations doing this

Groups like the Dian Fossey Gorilla Fund and the Gorilla Doctors run the veterinary and ranger work this recovery depends on.
